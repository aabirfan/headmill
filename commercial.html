Commercial mortgages and bridging loans explained.

Commercial mortgages fund business premises or investment property. Bridging loans provide short-term borrowing secured against property. This guide explains how they work and what lenders consider.

Types of borrowing

Finance for commercial property and development

The type of property, its use and the repayment plan affect the funding available. Each lender sets its own eligibility criteria and terms.

Commercial mortgages

A commercial mortgage is a loan secured against business premises or investment property. Business owners may use one to buy premises; investors may use one to buy property to let. Lenders assess the business's ability to repay or the property's rental income. Missed repayments can put the property used as security at risk.

Bridging loans

A bridging loan is short-term borrowing secured against property. Typical uses include auction purchases, refurbishment and a gap between buying and selling. Lenders need a credible repayment plan, often a property sale or longer-term refinancing.

Development finance

Development finance funds building projects, often with money released in stages as work progresses. Lenders assess the build budget, timetable and expected value on completion. Delays and unexpected costs can increase the amount needed.

Semi-commercial and mixed-use property

A shop with a flat above combines business and residential use. Its use, tenancy arrangements and income affect which lenders will consider it and the terms they may offer.

How it works

How lenders assess an application

1

Property and purpose

The lender considers the property, its intended use and the amount requested. A valuation helps establish the security available for the loan.

2

Finances and repayment

Business accounts, rental income and existing borrowing may form part of the assessment. For bridging loans, the lender checks whether the proposed sale or refinancing can repay the loan within its term.

3

Terms and costs

Terms set out the interest rate, fees and repayment schedule. Rates may be fixed or variable; variable rates can rise. Valuation, legal and arrangement fees can add to the cost. Indicative terms remain subject to formal approval.

4

Checks and completion

The lender completes its valuation and legal checks before releasing funds. Missing documents or issues with the property can delay completion, so funding is not guaranteed by a particular date.

Commercial questions

Commercial finance FAQs

What happens if a bridging loan cannot be repaid on time?

A delayed sale or unsuccessful refinance can leave the borrower unable to repay at the end of the term. An extension is not guaranteed and may add costs. The lender may take action against the property used as security.

How is bridging interest paid?

Depending on the terms, interest may be paid during the loan or added to the amount due at the end. Added interest increases the final repayment. The borrower needs to account for interest and fees as well as the original loan.

Can companies borrow?

Some lenders accept applications from limited companies and special purpose vehicles (companies set up for a specific purpose, such as holding property). Lender criteria vary. Legal and tax advice can help borrowers understand the implications of an ownership structure.

What deposit does a commercial mortgage need?

The deposit depends on the lender, the property and the borrower’s circumstances. The loan may not cover the full purchase price or all associated costs. A lower valuation can mean a larger deposit is needed.
